# ECCOOZS Reading Detective

## Visual + Interaction Architecture

The app should feel like **one world that matures with the learner**, not three unrelated games.

The core identity stays the same:

**Investigate → Collect Clues → Understand → Prove → Decide**

But the atmosphere changes with age.

---

# 1. Opening Screen

The first screen should be extremely simple.

### Logo

**Reading Detective**

### Subtitle

**Every word leaves clues.**

Then three large actions:

**Begin an Investigation**
 **Continue My Case**
 **Grown-Up / Educator**

No grade-level language on the front door.

No:

> Kindergarten
>  Grade 4
>  Reading Level 6

We don't want a struggling older learner seeing something immediately stigmatizing.

---

# 2. First-Time Onboarding

Keep it short.

### Screen 1

> **Welcome, Investigator.**

> Reading is about finding meaning.
>  We're going to discover which clues you already know and which ones we should investigate together.

Button:

**Start My First Case**

---

### Screen 2 — Presentation Age

Rather than:

> What reading level are you?

Ask:

> **Which experience feels right for you?**

The system can use age/account information when available, but the presentation should broadly map to:

**Young Investigator**
 **Investigator**
 **Lead Investigator**

We should probably avoid exposing “Elementary / Junior High / High School” here unless needed.

Those are curriculum stages internally.

---

# 3. The First Case Is the Placement Experience

This should be clever.

The learner thinks:

> I'm solving my first mystery.

The engine thinks:

> I'm building the initial reading evidence profile.

-  spoken clues 
-  labels 
-  words 
-  short sentences

---

## Junior presentation

### **The Missing Backpack**

Three students provide clues.

The learner reads:

-  locker label 
-  short message 
-  witness statement 
-  schedule 

---

## Older presentation

### **The Lost Delivery**

A package has gone missing.

Evidence includes:

-  delivery notification 
-  building notice 
-  text message 
-  simple timeline 
-  tracking update 

Same diagnostic engine.

Completely different dignity.

# 8. The Active Case

A case begins with a very short briefing.

Example:

# CASE 014

## The Changed Schedule

> Someone says the school event begins at 6:00 PM.

> But the posted notice may say something different.

### Your job:

**Determine when the event actually begins.**

That's immediately more interesting than:

> Read the paragraph and answer the following question.

---

# 9. Evidence Should Arrive Naturally

Instead of placing an entire worksheet on the screen, give learners pieces of information.

For example:

### Evidence 1

Text message:

> “I heard the event starts at six.”

### Evidence 2

Official notice:

> “Doors open at 6:00 PM. Program begins at 6:30 PM.”

### Evidence 3

Flyer:

> “Join us Friday evening!”

Now:

> **When does the program begin?**

Learner must distinguish:

**doors open**

from:

**program begins**

The detective theme is doing actual instructional work.

---

# 10. The Evidence Board

This could become one of the signature interfaces.

Evidence cards can be dragged into categories.

For Junior High:

**CONFIRMED**

**POSSIBLE**

**UNSUPPORTED**

**NEED MORE INFORMATION**

For High School:

**CLAIM**

**EVIDENCE**

**CONTRADICTION**

**CONDITION**

**EXCEPTION**

**UNKNOWN**

The board changes depending on the skill.

---

# 11. Evidence Connections

At more advanced levels, learners draw relationships.

Example:

**Claim:**
 The store closes at 8.

Connect to:

**Official hours:**
 Mon–Fri 9 AM–8 PM.

Connection:

**SUPPORTS**

But:

**Social post:**
 “Pretty sure they stay open until 9.”

Connection:

**CONFLICTS**

This visually teaches reasoning structure.

---

# 12. Clue Lab

When the diagnostic engine finds a gap, the learner briefly enters a focused environment.

Example:

# CLUE LAB

## The Word “Unless”

The lab should feel like:

> We found a tricky clue.

Not:

> You failed this concept.

The lesson may take only 3–6 minutes.

Then:

**Return to Case**

This reinforces the **Repair & Return** philosophy.

---

# 13. Elementary Clue Lab

Very visual.

Example:

### Sound clue: /m/

Learner hears:

**moon**

Then sees:

**moon**

Then isolates:

**m**

Then finds:

milk
 map
 sun

The environment feels exploratory, warm, tactile.

---

# 14. Junior High Clue Lab

More analytical.

Example:

## CONTRAST WORDS

Compare:

> Marcus wanted to attend. **However**, he had to work.

Then:

> Marcus wanted to attend. **Therefore**, he bought a ticket.

Learner visually sees:

**however → contrast**

**therefore → result**

Then applies it to the active case.

---

# 15. High School Clue Lab

Could feel almost like document analysis software.

Example:

## LEGAL CLUE: “WAIVE”

Clause:

> Participant waives the right to...

The interface breaks it into:

**WHO?**

Participant

**ACTION?**

Waives

**WHAT?**

The right to...

Then plain-language explanation.

Then another example.

Return to case.

This will make difficult language feel conquerable.

---

# 16. Detective Notebook

The notebook becomes a personalized reference guide.

Not a static textbook.

Every time the learner truly learns a tool, it appears in their notebook.

### Example page:

# CONDITION WORDS

**if**
 Something happens only when a condition is satisfied.

**unless**
 Something happens except when another condition applies.

**provided that**
 Something depends on a stated requirement.

Then:

### My reminder

> Look for the condition before deciding what the rule allows.

The notebook grows over time.

---

# 17. Learner-Created Notes

Eventually, Junior High and High School learners should be able to add their own notes.

Example:

> “May does NOT mean guaranteed.”

That is powerful.

Because learners begin externalizing their own reading strategies.

---

# 18. The Detective Companion

I think we need a guide character—but not a mascot who screams praise every 15 seconds.

More like a trusted mentor.

Their role:

-  model thinking 
-  ask questions 
-  explain misconceptions 
-  reduce help over time 

For Elementary, the mentor is present frequently.

Junior High:

less frequently.

High School:

appears mostly when requested or when the system detects a real breakdown.

Eventually:

> **The Detective steps back.**

That has symbolic value.

---

# 19. We Should Have More Than One Character

The world would feel richer if learners encounter recurring people.

For example:

### Lead Mentor

Calm, thoughtful, encouraging.

### Research Specialist

Helps with sources and evidence.

### Language Specialist

Appears in vocabulary and legal-language investigations.

### Technical Specialist

Appears in diagrams, procedures and science cases.

### Community Investigator

Appears in neighborhood/civic cases.

All FBA representation should feel natural and sophisticated.

### Literature Cases

This allows enormous content variety.

# 29. Evidence Highlighting

Highlighting should be a core interaction.

Learner receives:

> “Show the sentence that supports your conclusion.”

They highlight it directly.

Then:

> “Which words inside that sentence matter most?”

Highlight again.

Example:

> “Employees **may request** schedule changes.”

The learner highlights:

**may request**

This teaches precision.

---

# 30. Evidence Zoom

At advanced levels:

First select the **source**.

Then select the **sentence**.

Then select the **words**.

That gives us a hierarchy:

**Source → Passage → Sentence → Phrase**

This is excellent for source reasoning.

---

# 31. “Show Your Evidence”

This should become the app's signature prompt.

Not every question.

But frequently.

> **What do you think?**

Learner answers.

Then:

> **Show your evidence.**

Eventually the learner automatically starts looking for evidence before answering.

That is exactly what we want.

---

# 32. Wrong Answer Experience

Imagine learner chooses:

> “The customer gets a refund.”

Instead of:

❌ WRONG

The interface says:

> **Let's inspect the agreement again.**

It highlights:

> “Prepaid charges are nonrefundable.”

Then asks:

> “Which word changes the result?”

The learner selects:

**nonrefundable**

The correction itself becomes instruction.

---

# 33. When the Learner Changes Their Mind

We should celebrate this.

A case reveals new evidence.

Learner revises their conclusion.

Message:

> **Good investigators update conclusions when evidence changes.**

That is a fantastic habit to reinforce.

Not:

> “Your first answer was wrong.”

---

# 34. “Insufficient Evidence” Must Be a Real Option

As we discussed:

**CONFIRMED**

**LIKELY**

**POSSIBLE**

**INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE**

This should appear increasingly often.

And sometimes:

**Insufficient Evidence** is the strongest answer.

# 42. Focus Mode

This could be extremely valuable.

When enabled:

Everything except the current sentence or evidence card fades back.

No clutter.

Then:

**Next**

The learner advances deliberately.

Especially useful for:

-  attention differences 
-  visual processing 
-  beginning readers 
-  complex legal text

# 43. Read-Aloud Interaction

Tap sentence:

🔊 sentence read.

Tap individual word:

🔊 word read **only when instruction permits it**.

During decoding assessment, the target word may intentionally not be voiced.

The app needs to know what skill is being measured before giving help.

---

# 44. Vocabulary Popovers

At Junior High and High School, the learner can select an unfamiliar word.

Instead of instantly giving the answer:

### First prompt

> “Want to investigate it?”

Options:

**Break the Word Apart**

**Use Context**

**Hear It**

**Show Meaning**

This teaches strategy before dependency.

---

# 45. Legal Word Interaction

Select:

**indemnify**

Popover:

### LEGAL TERM

**Plain-language meaning**

**Who does it affect in this clause?**

**What action does it create?**

**Read full clause**

This reinforces that legal words must be understood **in context**.

---

# 46. The Interface Should Teach Scanning

For longer High School documents:

Search box.

Headings.

Table of contents.

Defined-term navigation.

The learner might be told:

> “Find the cancellation deadline.”

They shouldn't have to read 1,500 words linearly.

Learning how to locate information is part of advanced literacy.

# 48. Multi-Source Workspace

Advanced investigations:

Three source tabs.

### Source A

Official document

# 50. Final Graduation Experience

This should be a real event.

Not:

> Test 60 Questions

# THE FINAL CASE

They receive:

-  article 
-  email 
-  policy 
-  chart 
-  advertisement 
-  witness account 
-  agreement excerpt 
-  timeline 

The case contains:

-  relevant evidence 
-  irrelevant details

-  conflicting claims 
-  qualifying language

# 51. The Mentor Should Barely Speak During the Final Case

This matters symbolically.

The learner is now doing the work.

At the end, the Mentor can say something like:

> **You didn't just solve the case. You knew what to look for.**

Then:

# CASE CLOSED

### Lead Investigator

And the learner's Notebook remains available forever.
